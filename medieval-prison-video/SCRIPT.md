# Why You Wouldn't Survive a Medieval Prison: script and shot list

## Full narration

London. The summer of thirteen twenty-two. A crowded market. A shout. A purse has been cut. Heads turn. A finger points. At you. You didn't do it. Right now, that doesn't matter. Within the hour, you're marched to the city's western gate. Not through it. Into it. Because this gate is a prison. They call it Newgate. And here's what most people get wrong about medieval prisons. The thing most likely to kill you in here isn't a torturer. It's hunger. It's fever. It's the crowd. It's the bill. And, above all, it's the wait.

### Chapter I: You're Arrested

First, forget modern prisons. A medieval gaol was mostly a waiting room. It held people until something else happened. A trial. A payment. A pardon. Or a rope. An old Roman principle, repeated by the English lawbook known as Bracton, said prison was for holding people, not punishing them. In practice, that line was very blurry. Around you are accused felons, awaiting the king's justices. Steal anything worth more than a shilling, and you could hang. Then there are debtors. Under Edward the First's laws, a merchant could have a debtor locked up until he paid. The law did promise him bread and water, quote, to the end that he die not in prison for default of sustenance. Bread. And water. That was the safety net. Now, the big question. Do you have money? Money and friends could buy bail, or a better prison. London later kept Ludgate for, quote, citizens and other reputable persons. You are not reputable. You're going to Newgate.

### Chapter II: Your First Night

The passage narrows. Daylight shrinks to a grey slit behind you. The keeper walks ahead, keys swinging. Then the smell hits. Smoke. Sweat. And the latrine everyone shares. Newgate is built into a city gate. The windows are small, because big windows are how people leave. The stone is cold, even in summer. The straw is damp. And not only with water. Somewhere in the dark, someone is coughing. Someone is praying. Chains shift whenever a body moves. The keeper fixes an iron ring around your ankle. Lighter irons? Certainly. For a fee. Welcome to the economy of prison.

### Chapter III: Food Wasn't Free

Here's what surprises people. The prison didn't really feed you. Many keepers bought or rented their jobs, and earned it back from prisoners. Fees to come in. Fees for a bed. Fees for lighter chains. The historian Ralph Pugh argued these fees weren't simply an abuse of the system. They were how the system worked. So who pays for your food? You do. If you have money. If not, your family, bringing bread to the grate. If they can spare it. Then, charity. Londoners left money in their wills for Newgate's prisoners. And in thirteen sixteen, during a great famine, when a baker's loaves were found short weight, the mayor's court confiscated them, and gave them to the prisoners in Newgate. So your dinner depends on somebody else getting caught cheating. And when none of it comes? In May thirteen twenty-two, Thomas atte Grene was held in Newgate over a hundred shillings he owed. The coroner's record says he, quote, died of starvation in the said prison. Not by violence. Not for any crime. He simply ran out of food.

### Chapter IV: Disease

Now add people. Lots of people. Almost no fresh air. One latrine. Water that may not be clean. Lice. Fleas. Rats. Records rarely name the disease. In London, the coroner viewed prisoners who died in gaol, and the same phrases appear again and again. He died his rightful death. The corpse viewed, on which no hurt appeared. Rightful death meant natural causes. Page after page of them. Later English jails became notorious for gaol fever, probably typhus. How often it struck medieval prisons, we can't be sure. But in fourteen nineteen, London closed Ludgate, and moved its prisoners into Newgate. Within months, the city reversed itself. Its own ordinance says that, because of the fetid and corrupt atmosphere of the hateful gaol of Newgate, many of them were now dead. People who, it admitted, might have been living. Money left by Dick Whittington, yes, that one, later paid to rebuild it.

### Chapter V: Not Just Dungeons

And the deep, dripping dungeon? Sometimes true. Usually not. A prison was wherever somebody with authority could lock a door. County gaols were often inside royal castles, like York. Towns used their gatehouses. Political enemies went to the Tower. Across the river, in Southwark, stood the Marshalsea, the King's Bench, and the Clink. For small offences, you didn't even need a building. After the Black Death, every town was ordered to make stocks, for workers who demanded higher wages. Different places. Different rules. The same lock.

### Chapter VI: The Way Out

So. You decide to get out. You wouldn't be the first. In eleven-oh-one, Ranulf Flambard, the Tower's first known prisoner, threw a feast for his guards. Once they were drunk, he climbed down a rope, smuggled in, the story goes, in a jug of wine. In twelve forty-four, the Welsh prince Gruffudd ap Llywelyn knotted a rope from sheets, hangings and tablecloths. It broke. He fell to his death. And the chronicler Matthew Paris drew the moment. In thirteen twenty-three, Roger Mortimer's guards were drugged at a feast. He slipped through the kitchens, over the wall, and into a boat. Within four years, he'd helped overthrow the king. And in thirteen twenty-five, prisoners in Newgate itself broke out, claimed sanctuary in a church, and were allowed to leave the kingdom for good. But most famous escapes belong to the powerful. People with friends, money, and help inside. You have a stone wall, an iron ring, and a keeper who could be fined if you vanished. The one person in this building who most wants you to stay, is the one holding the key.

### Chapter VII: The Wait

So you stay. At least the trial will be soon. Right? Here's the catch. Felony cases waited until royal justices came to deliver the gaol. A statute of thirteen thirty said that should happen at least three times a year. That it needed saying tells you something. Magna Carta had promised: to no one will we sell, to no one deny or delay right or justice. But justice ran on sessions, sheriffs, juries and witnesses. Cases were adjourned. Accusers didn't turn up. Approvers, criminals who'd accused their accomplices, could wait a very long time while every accusation was tried. Some never saw a courtroom. In July thirteen twenty-two, a man arrested for cutting a purse, just like you, died in Newgate. No hurt was found on him. And if your day comes, there's the trial. Felony trials were decided by juries. But you had to agree to be tried. Refuse, and a statute of twelve seventy-five prescribed prison forte et dure. Harsh imprisonment, on a starvation diet, until you gave in. In thirteen fifty-seven, Cecily de Ridgeway, accused of killing her husband, refused to plead. She reportedly went forty days without food or drink. King Edward the Third pardoned her. It was treated as a miracle. That's what it took. A miracle.

### Final section

So. Would you survive a medieval prison? Probably not because of the rack, or the red-hot iron. The real danger was slower. No money for food. No air. A fever moving from body to body. And a trial date nobody could promise. Poverty. Hunger. Disease. Crowds. Uncertainty. On its own, each one was survivable. Together, they killed people who were never convicted of anything. We picture the Middle Ages as castles, candlelight and banners. But for many ordinary people, the most dangerous place in the medieval world was a small, dark room, where they were told to wait.
