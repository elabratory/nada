# Research notes — "Why You Wouldn't Survive a Medieval Prison"

Every factual claim in the narration is listed here with its evidence. Confidence:
**High** = primary source checked directly or consensus of standard scholarship;
**Medium** = reliable secondary source, wording in the script hedged accordingly.

| # | Claim (as used in narration) | Evidence | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A man held in Newgate in July 1322 after being arrested for cutting a purse died there; "no hurt appeared" on the body. | Inquest on John le Ratelere, "attached for cutting off the purse of John de Pelham", died in Newgate "his rightful death" before the Feast of St Margaret, 16 Edw. II. | R. R. Sharpe (ed.), *Calendar of Coroners Rolls of the City of London, 1300–1378* (1913), Roll E, no. 39 | High |
| 2 | Medieval gaols mainly held people until trial, payment or punishment; prison was, in principle, for holding not punishing. | Maxim *carcer ad continendos homines, non ad puniendos haberi debet* (Ulpian, Digest 48.19.8.9), repeated in the 13th-c. treatise *Bracton*. | 3 | Theft of goods worth more than 12d (one shilling) was a capital felony (grand larceny). | 12d threshold defined by statute under Edward I (prob. 1278); Blackstone summarises the rule. | Blackstone, *Commentaries* IV ch. 17; Cambridge Core literature on grand larceny | High |

## Rejected / not used
- "In 1414 the keepers of Newgate and Ludgate and 64 prisoners died": appears in a later chronicle; I could not verify the source, so it's omitted.
- "62 deaths in Newgate 1315–16": reported by secondary writers from an unpublished roll; I couldn't check it, so it's omitted.
- "Garnish" (prisoners' entry money): a 16th–18th-century term, so it's avoided.
